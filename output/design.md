# Campus Customs — Design Pass (Problem 10)

What we changed to make the site feel like a real storefront, and why each
change helps a shopper stay and buy. We kept the Yale brand palette (navy
`#00366b`, cool neutrals) and added character through type, motion, and detail.

## Type
- **Fraunces** (a heritage display serif) for headings, **Inter** for UI text.
  Why: the serif reads established and collegiate, fitting a 1973 New Haven shop;
  Inter keeps prices, sizes, and buttons crisp and legible. A distinctive,
  trustworthy typeface makes the store feel real rather than templated.

## Storefront trust cues
- **Announcement bar** across the top: "Printed & embroidered in New Haven ·
  Family-run since 1973 · Pickup at 57 Broadway." Why: real shops lead with where
  and how they make things; it builds trust and sets expectations in one line.
- **Brand monogram** ("CC") in the nav, and a richer **three-column footer**
  (shop links, address, story). Why: a consistent mark and a real footer make the
  site feel like a business, and the shop links keep people browsing.

## Motion (subtle, and disabled under reduced-motion)
- Hero text and sections **fade up** on load; buttons lift on hover; the chat
  panel **slides in**; messages **ease in**. Why: gentle motion signals quality
  and guides the eye without getting in the way. It is fully turned off for
  visitors who prefer reduced motion, so it never hurts accessibility.

## Product presentation
- Cards now **zoom the photo slightly on hover**, show a **category chip**, and a
  **"View details →"** cue. Why: the hover makes the grid feel alive and
  invites clicks; the chip and cue tell shoppers what each item is and that there
  is more to see, nudging them into the product page where they buy.

## Chat feel
- The assistant now has a **"CC" avatar**, a pulsing **online dot**, and a
  "usually replies in a few seconds" line, plus message animations. Why: it reads
  like chatting with a real shop employee, which makes people more comfortable
  asking for help, and help is what turns a browser into a buyer.

## Net effect
The site now looks made-with-care and alive, which keeps shoppers browsing
longer, and every touch points toward the two actions that matter: opening a
product and asking the assistant for help.
